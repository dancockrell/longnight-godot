# The Long Night — what this game actually is

One page, written to force a straight answer to "what is your concept",
not a design document. If a sentence here needs a diagram to make sense,
the sentence is wrong.

## The pitch

**You are a time traveller whose entire job is to be forgotten twice: once
by the era you're standing in, and once by the machine that sent you.**

You work for Project 42's Chrono pillar out of Camp Iron Bell, 1944. The
programme can only retrieve people history already loses — it cannot touch
anyone the record is certain about, because the record is never wrong.
That one rule is the whole game. It means:

- **Every place you're sent is a place already defined by an absence.**
  You go to 1888 London *because* Whitechapel in the autumn of the
  murders is a place where the record is full of holes on purpose — a
  city looking hard at one thing so it doesn't have to look at the rest.
- **You can't fix what you find there.** The Consistency Finding means
  the loop is already closed. Catherine Eddowes's apron gets found, the
  chalk gets sponged off the wall at dawn, no photograph exists. You
  cannot stop it, because if you could, it would already not have
  happened. The only thing in your power is what *you* personally manage
  to carry out of the room.
- **So the actual verb of the game is not "fight." It's "record."**
  Witnessing something does nothing. A thing you don't write down is
  gone the moment you are. The whole design — Exposure, the Ledger, the
  three-way disagreement between what different people hold to be true —
  is one idea wearing three hats: **memory is the resource, and the game
  is entirely about what gets preserved and what doesn't, running in
  both directions at once.** What the era's own record preserves about
  its people, and what you manage to preserve of what you saw.

## The two costs that run the whole game

**Exposure — noise.** Programme technology in a period with no
vocabulary for it gets noticed. Cover rebuilds; a permanent *record* of
your anachronism never does. Go loud and you might win the fight in
front of you; you also just handed the next 90 years a document that
was never supposed to exist.

**The Ledger — memory.** Independent of Exposure. What you saw and did
nothing with is gone. What you wrote down survives exactly as long as
its medium does, and the people who want it gone get a vote too — a
wall gets sponged, a file gets burned, a notebook in your own coat
pocket does not, because nobody with the authority to erase it knows it
exists.

Combat exists — the old canvas engine's threat/stance system is worth
keeping — but it is not the point. It is one of the ways you spend
Exposure, not the win condition. **Dracula was never a health bar and
never will be one here: the final reckoning of any era is a tally of what
you preserved, not a boss fight you clear.**

## Who you play

Six invented people, never real ones — the Ethical Constitution is
absolute on that point and the design doesn't need an exception. Each is
a different, incompatible answer to "what is Camp Iron Bell entitled to
do?" You start as one. The rest get recruited as the eras go on, and the
party's internal argument about what the programme is doing to history —
and to them — is the actual character drama, not a stat spread.

## What makes this not-generic

A hundred time-travel RPGs let you change the past and then spend the
whole game finding out that was a mistake. This one starts from the
opposite premise and never lets you forget it: **you cannot change
anything, ever, and the entire tension is what you do with a set of
facts you already know the ending to.** The tragedy isn't the twist. It's
the starting condition, stated on page one, and the game is what a person
does with it anyway.

## What "nice" means for this specific game

Not spectacle. The bible's own art direction — a flat, honest period
layer with something uncanny showing through the seam — is the right
instinct for this game too, at a much smaller budget: plain typewritten
or handwritten documents, an actual sense of *place* per era (Camp Iron
Bell should feel like humming fluorescent-lit paperwork; Whitechapel
should feel like cold and fog and gaslight), and sound that does real
work instead of decorating — the bible specifies a 60Hz mains hum under
Project 42 because the camp runs on more current than it can explain.
That is a one-line spec for real ambient audio, and it costs nothing to
build procedurally.
